Why the rule said kill: 0.71 against 0.54, and what those numbers are

codestories · the accuracy measure (H1) · how it was done

    What was measured

    __DEN_S__story contradicted claims per 1,000 words
    __DEN_D__deepwiki contradicted claims per 1,000 words

    raw: __RAW_S__ vs __RAW_D__ contradicted, of 360 sampled claims each · the rule needed story ≤ ½ × deepwiki

    H1 was the core claim of the project: that a story whose every sentence links to pinned lines, with a proof per chapter, would contradict the code far less often than generated documentation. "Far less" was fixed in advance as half the rate or better. The measured rate came out higher, not lower. This explainer shows how the rate was produced, why it came out that way, and what it does and doesn't mean.

    A "contradicted claim" is a sentence about the code that the code shows to be false. The rate is per 1,000 words of text, so a long wiki and a short story can be compared. Each piece of that definition is a design decision, and each gets a screen.

    Turning prose into checkable claims

    You can't check a paragraph. You can check "Response.ok is True if status_code is between 200 and 299." So the first step rewrites each text into atomic claims: one fact each, standing alone, with "it" and "this" resolved. The same prompt did this for both arms. It keeps behaviour, names, values, defaults, conditions and order; it drops narrative, advice, opinion, and anything about tests, CI or project history.

      __WIKI_CLAIMS__

    Two numbers come out of this step and both matter later: how many claims a text makes, and how many words it took. The story arm made about __CPW_S__ claims per 1,000 words, DeepWiki about __CPW_D__. Stories are denser in checkable statements: a concrete telling has more places to be right or wrong.

    Forty per arm per repository

    Judging every claim would have meant about 8,000 judgments. The preregistration said: sample up to 40 claims per repository per arm, with a fixed random seed, so the sample is the same on any rerun and nobody can pick it. That's 360 story claims and 360 DeepWiki claims across the nine repositories, 720 in all.

    The rate is then scaled back up: if 2 of 40 sampled claims are contradicted and the text made 489 claims in 22,131 words, the estimate is (2 ÷ 40) × (489 ÷ 22,131 × 1,000) = 1.10 contradicted claims per 1,000 words. Screen 7 lets you work that formula.

    Sampling has a cost that shows up in screen 8: with 40 draws, the difference between 0 and 2 contradicted is one or two sentences, and the interval around the final ratio is wide.

    Who judged, and what it saw

    The judge is claude-opus-5 through the API, same as every other role: no tools, no fine-tuning, fresh conversation per batch. It was given the repository's source at the commit the arm describes, and a numbered list of 20 claims, and asked to label each contradicted, supported or unverifiable, citing the lines that decide it.

    Blind in two ways. The 80 sampled claims of a repository, both arms, were shuffled together before batching, so a batch mixes story and wiki claims and the judge is never told which is which. And the instruction was symmetric: "a claim that is true but imprecise is supported; a claim that is wrong in any specific is contradicted," whatever the source.

    A second model, Jev (TypeSafe's, on Cloudflare: a different vendor), then re-scored each claim from only the claim and the lines Claude cited, as a probability of contradiction. Jev agreed with Claude's label on __JEV__ of the claims it could score. Claude's label decides; Jev is the check on a shared blind spot.

    The judge was tested before it was trusted

    A judge that calls everything "supported" would make both arms look perfect. So before the comparison, it was tested on known answers: 10 true claims per arm from the in-sample repository, each paired with a false twin, the same sentence with one specific changed. The 40 were shuffled and judged blind. The passing bar was written down first: every lie caught, at most one truth wrongly flagged, per arm.

    __TWINS__

    Result: __JT__. That is the evidence that the judge can tell a lie from a truth when it can see the code. It is not evidence that the judge could see the code in every repository, which is the next screen.

    The judge was run twice, and the first time it was half blind

    The judge reads the repository as a block of files that has to fit a budget. Without a trace to say which files matter, that block led with the README and docs, and in the large repositories the budget ran out before the source: for rich the judge saw 1 of about 80 modules. It did the honest thing and said "unverifiable: that file was not shown." But unverifiable counts as not contradicted in the metric. The first pass found almost nothing wrong with the stories, because it couldn't see the code they were about, and the rule said ship.

    show pass:

    The fix put the source files the claims name first, then the rest of the package, then docs; it was checked without the model by listing what each batch would see. The judge re-ran on the same 720 claims; nothing else was re-run. Both passes are published, the change is logged in the preregistration's deviations, and the change can only move claims out of "unverifiable", so it could only make the story look worse or the same. It made it look worse.

    Why the story loses on the rate with nearly the same raw count

    rate = (contradicted ÷ sampled) × (claims extracted ÷ words × 1000)
    story: (__RAW_S__ ÷ 360) × (__EXT_S__ ÷ __W_S__ × 1000) = __DEN_S__
    deepwiki: (__RAW_D__ ÷ 360) × (__EXT_D__ ÷ __W_D__ × 1000) = __DEN_D__

    Ten versus seven wrong sentences is close. The gap in the rate comes from the second factor: the story packs more checkable claims into each thousand words, so the same proportion of wrong claims is more wrong claims per word. The metric was preregistered that way on purpose, as a rate per word of what a reader reads, and it stands. But it is worth seeing what it rewards.     Two things stand out. The story's errors are small misstatements of mechanism in otherwise accurate paragraphs, and every one sits in a chapter whose citation and proof checks passed: those checks pin lines and values, they don't read the sentence. DeepWiki's errors include two about CI workflow files and one about line numbers, kinds of claim a story never makes.

    Could it be the sample?

    Ten versus seven, out of 360 each, is a difference of three sentences. The preregistered bootstrap resamples whole repositories (some twice, some not at all), recomputes both rates and their ratio, and repeats. Draw and watch.

    The preregistered run (10,000 draws, fixed seed) gave a 95% interval for the ratio of __CI0__ to __CI1__. It crosses ½ (where the story would have passed) and 1 (where the two arms are equal). Statistically, nine repositories and 40 claims each cannot tell these two arms apart on accuracy. The rule was written to use the point estimate, and the point estimate is 1.3: on the wrong side of ½ by a wide margin. So the rule says kill, and the interval says the honest sentence is "no better," not "worse."

    What you can say, and what you can't

    Not more accurate, and not shown to be lessThe point estimate is against the story; the interval includes equality. "The stories were not more accurate than DeepWiki's pages" is the sentence; "were less accurate" is not supported.
    The checks worked as built, and that wasn't enoughAll 76 chapters passed citation, drift and proof checks. Ten sampled sentences were still wrong about the code. The pipeline's own paragraph judge (Jev at a 0.7 cut-off) caught 4 of those 10. The eval's judge, with the source in view, caught all 10. That judge exists and is tested; the prototype didn't use it inside the pipeline.
    Omoju's hand check20 judged claims, arm hidden: 18 agreed with the judge. Both disagreements were "unverifiable" labels on claims about test files, which the judge doesn't read; none concerned a contradicted or supported label.
    One extraction was chunkedrich's DeepWiki text was too long for one output and was extracted in three page-aligned chunks; it yielded 74 claims per 1,000 words against 17–31 elsewhere. That raises DeepWiki's pooled claims-per-word and so raises DeepWiki's rate; without rich, DeepWiki's rate would be __DEN_D_NORICH__ and the story's __DEN_S_NORICH__. The direction of the result does not change.
    Same model family throughoutThe judge is a Claude model, as is the author. Jev, from another vendor, agreed on __JEV__ of the claims it scored; that and the hand check are the guards.

    The sentence that survives: on nine small Python libraries, a blind model judge reading the code found CodeStories no more accurate than DeepWiki's pages (0.71 vs 0.54 contradicted claims per 1,000 words; 10 vs 7 of 360 sampled; 95% interval on the ratio 0.55–4.86). The checks a story carries, that its lines exist, haven't changed, and its proofs run, did not make its sentences more accurate than generated documentation. The preregistered rule required half the rate; it said kill.
